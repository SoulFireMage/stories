From a lightyear away, he folded space and poked his nose through, entered the dock then landed in a quiet secure part of the station in silence, without notice. At twenty five meters from nose to tail—tiny compared to most ships at his home station—his slim sleek pearlescent shape was lost amongst the bustle, the hint of fractal rainbow shades not revealing his presence. Reaching into a space fold and pulling out a shipsuit, he slipped it on and wandered back into the station. Landing in a cleared part of the dock let him skip Processing and Customs. 

It never failed to give him a thrill, to unfold and transform into the Falcon, his Ship self.  The ship was him, and he was the ship and had been so for a decade now and no one was the wiser. A few close calls but so far he'd been lucky. Known as the Messenger by his clients, his business as a discrete courier had grown steadily over the years. Never taking passengers, only parcels and letters—always guaranteed to arrive at the fastest possible speed. People expected, at best, a two week trip to the next nearest Waystation and leaving him plenty of time to take more work. 

His best time to Cluso Clark Station, if he factored in time to change, a rushed meal and drink, was maybe half an hour. Usually substantially less. The fun part? Time did not expand with distance. 

It took him a long time to come to terms with the sensation of folding space: each fold, executed with precision by his starship self, sent a shiver through his mind, yet the ship part of his psyche didn’t waver. His sensors would flicker as the void pulsed with a cold, unyielding darkness, and for one long, electrified moment, even his mechanized limbs would hesitate. Gradually, this folding became routine— over time, shifting from terror, through anxiety then eventually acceptance—until space became the sky to his falcon, his natural second home.

Walking through the station, he approached the Yoko bar to meet his next drop off. The lead lined slim box in his hand contained doses of an antiviral medication for Tharion Mosaic virus. Once considered incurable, the synthetic dna virus was one hundred percent fatal unless treated within a month. His clients were sitting at a discrete booth at the back of the dim lit bar, the twilight shaded space a little murky and muffled—though completely transparent to Gawain, just like the treatment vials in the lead lined pouch. Folding space as a ship involved a sense that did not turn off easily. “Here it is,” he said.
